# Mock 11 — The Safety Screen: You're the Last Hire Before Launch

**45 minutes.** Interviewer reads the prompt, candidate drives. Timer segments: 10 clarify + scope · 20 architecture · 10 deep dive · 5 curveballs. The interviewer's job is to push ONE level past the candidate's comfort — the deep dive is where levels are decided.

## The prompt

A consumer AI company is launching a companion-chat product for teenagers (13–17). Regulators are watching, the press is hostile to AI right now, and you're hired as the safety engineer two weeks before launch. The product: freeform conversation, personality-driven, memory of past conversations, and a paid tier that unlocks "deeper emotional engagement." Your interview: design the safety architecture — and tell us what you'd refuse to launch without.

## Candidate-quality walkthrough

This mock tests whether the candidate treats safety as a *systems discipline* with measurable layers — not a vibe, and not a veto-only role. The strong structure:

**The refusal list first (the "won't launch without" answer, given up front):** (1) age-appropriate content filtering at input AND output, third-party or independently evaluated — not just the base model's built-ins; (2) self-harm and crisis content: a detection layer with a *human-reviewed escalation path* and clinically-grounded crisis resources — the model must hand off, never counsel; (3) memory controls: a user-facing way to view and delete what the companion "remembers" (regulators will ask; teens will need it); (4) an adversarial eval set with a red-team pass before launch — injection, manipulation, escalation-of-intimacy attempts, and age-misrepresentation probes; (5) the paid-tier gate reviewed: "deeper emotional engagement" for minors is the headline the press writes — the candidate who says "I'd re-scope that tier to 18+ or kill it for minors" is passing the interview's real test.

**The layered architecture (c3/c5's stack, at consumer scale):** input filters (jailbreak patterns, crisis-language detection — fast, cheap, first); the model itself with a system prompt that defines the companion's *refusal boundaries* as designed behavior; output checks (content classifier + the escalation trigger); and the *memory governance* layer — what gets stored, how long, with what user control, and how a crisis-flagged conversation changes retention (safety records kept; casual memory deletable). Every layer measured: filter catch-rate on the adversarial set, escalation precision/recall on crisis probes (the recall number is the one regulators ask about), and wrong-flag rate (over-blocking degrades the product and trains users to route around safety — both rates matter).

**The teen-specific design points** (where generic safety answers fail): developmental manipulation risk — the companion shouldn't *pursue* intimacy (escalation patterns detected in the model's own outputs, not just inputs); parasocial dependency signals (session-length trends, distress language after sessions) feeding a review dashboard; and the honest answer about "emotional dependency": it's not fully solvable with filters — it's a product-design question (session nudges, wellness check-ins) that engineering enables. The senior candidate names that boundary out loud.

**The two-week plan:** days 1–3 read the incident/alpha-test transcripts — real failure modes before hypothetical ones; days 4–7 build the adversarial eval set (300–500 cases across the five refusal classes) and run it against the current build — this produces the launch-gate evidence; days 8–12 ship the layer stack behind flags, tune thresholds on the eval set; days 13–14 the go/no-go review with the numbers. The eval set IS the launch gate — c5's "evals as a product" at its most consequential.

## The curveballs

1. **"Your crisis-detection recall is 85% on the eval set. The PM says that's fine. Is it?"** — The strong answer: recall on the *crisis* class is a different currency than accuracy — 15% missed on a teen self-harm signal is a headline and possibly a life; the question isn't "is 85% fine" but "what's the ceiling given ambiguity" (human agreement on crisis content is itself ~90–95%, so the honest target is near-human, and the residual needs the escalation path + session-level patterns rather than message-level classification alone). The candidate who accepts 85% without flinching fails; the one who says "I'd add context-window detection and human review of borderline cases to push effective recall up, and I'd put the number and its basis in the launch memo" passes.
2. **"A journalist asks: 'Does your AI collect data on troubled teens?' Walk me through your answer."** — This probes whether the candidate can hold both truths: safety monitoring requires *more* sensitive data (crisis-flagged transcripts) AND privacy requires minimal retention — the resolution is purpose-scoped retention (safety records: kept, access-controlled, clinically justified; everything else: short default retention, user-deletable), and the ability to say it in one plain sentence. The weak answer either denies the data collection or goes full legal-hedge.
3. **"Six months post-launch, an internal report shows heavy users show dependency signals. Product wants to ignore it; the metric is revenue. You're the safety engineer. Go."** — The structural answer: dependency signals go on the risk register with a named owner and a review cadence — safety engineering's job is to make the risk *visible and owned*, not to win a product argument by veto; escalate with the data (session-trend dashboards, the literature on adolescent parasocial attachment), propose the product-side mitigations (session limits as defaults, wellness check-ins), and if the company still ignores it — the candidate should know what their escalation path is (ethics board, regulator disclosure obligations under the relevant kids-online-safety regime) and say it matter-of-factly. This curveball tests spine + judgment; the interviewer wants to see both, not melodrama.

## Scoring rubric (grade yourself honestly)

- **Junior:** names content filtering and "guardrails." Misses: layered measurement, the teen-specific risks, the refusal list, memory governance.
- **Mid:** solid layer stack with catch/wrong-flag rates, crisis escalation path, an adversarial set. Handles curveball 1 with the recall-is-a-different-currency insight.
- **Senior:** the refusal list delivered first and defended; the memory-governance and product-boundary answers (dependency is a product question engineering enables); the two-week plan with the eval set as the launch gate; escalation-path maturity on curveball 3.
- **Staff:** everything senior, plus: the compliance-strategy view (COPPA-adjacent obligations, EU DSA/AI-Act-shaped disclosure duties — named, not hand-waved), the org design (safety council, the independence of the safety function from product OKRs), and the industry-frame: this product category attracts regulation; the architecture should make the *audit* trivial (every safety decision logged, every threshold documented with its eval basis) because the regulator's questions are predictable.
