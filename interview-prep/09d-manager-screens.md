# Behavioral Screens — The Manager Round (09d)

**The round nobody preps for.** The manager/hiring-manager screen is not the panel behavioral: it's one hour with the person who will own your performance reviews, and it tests a different axis — judgment under ambiguity, upward management, and whether you'll make THEIR life better or harder. These ten questions (M-1..10) are the ones manager screens actually ask. Shape answers with STAR (09a) but calibrate to the manager's listener: they're grading 'would I put this person in front of my boss' as much as 'can they build.'

---

## M-1 — "Walk me through a project from your last role."

**Probing:** structure and ownership — do you narrate like an owner or a participant?

**Strong shape:** 90 seconds end-to-end (context, your specific role stated plainly, the outcome with numbers), then STOP and let them pick the thread. Managers are drowning in candidates who monologue; the confident summary plus a comfortable silence is the tell of someone who reports well.

## M-2 — "How do you like to be managed?"

**Probing:** self-knowledge and honesty calibrated to survivable levels.

**Strong shape:** real preferences with workarounds attached ("I do best with written context before discussions — I've learned to ask for the doc 24h ahead; async-heavy managers and I click"). The honest-with-boundaries answer beats both the flattery answer ("whatever works for you!") and the requirements-list answer (six needs, no flexibility).

## M-3 — "Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager."

**Probing:** the boss's-eye view of conflict — will you make disagreement expensive?

**Strong shape:** the disagreement stated fairly (their position in its best form — Mock 11's flat-tone rule), the evidence you brought, your handling of the outcome EITHER way. What managers listen for: did you disagree ONCE, well, and then commit — or campaign? The sentence that lands: "I made the case with data, they decided differently, I built their decision as well as I'd have built mine — and six months later we looked at the number together either way."

## M-4 — "What's the biggest mistake you've made at work?"

**Probing:** ownership range — does 'my mistake' mean your typo or your project?

**Strong shape:** a real one with real cost (STAR story 1's leak, or a production miss), the fix, and the systemic change. The manager-version addition: name what you told your manager and WHEN. Covering up is the disqualifier; a mistake reported early is a trust-building event.

## M-5 — "How do you handle a teammate who isn't pulling their weight?"

**Probing:** are you a manager's asset (early signal, low drama) or a liability (endgame escalation, resentment)?

**Strong shape:** direct-then-escalate with the sequence explicit: talk to them first (offer help — often it's a blocked/sick/misunderstood-scope situation), then surface to the manager EARLY framed as a project risk, not a person complaint. The sentence: "my job is making the risk visible while it's cheap to fix; your job is the personnel call — I don't want to do your job, I want to make it easy."

## M-6 — "Where do you want to be in two years?"

**Probing:** will this role hold you, and do your ambitions fit the org's shape?

**Strong shape:** direction, not title-demanding ("running systems with more blast radius, and mentoring — the track this role is on") plus honest openness about what you're still learning. The anti-answer: "your job" (joke, adds nothing) or a five-year plan with dates (reads as either naive or inflexible).

## M-7 — "Tell me about the best manager you've had."

**Probing:** you're describing what you'll optimize for — and whether you can articulate good management at all.

**Strong shape:** specific behaviors, not vibes ("gave me the ugly context early and let me own the call" beats "really supportive"), and what YOU did with it. If every manager you've had was terrible, this question is where it shows — and the manager listening knows their colleagues are your references' peers.

## M-8 — "How do you prioritize when everything is urgent?"

**Probing:** judgment mechanics under pressure, and whether you make your manager arbitrate everything (expensive) or nothing (risky).

**Strong shape:** the triage frame (reversible-vs-irreversible, blast radius, who's blocked by each thing), ONE concrete example, and the escalation protocol: "when two immovable priorities collide, I bring you a one-liner with my recommendation and what I'd need to be wrong — you make the call in 30 seconds instead of 30 minutes."

## M-9 — "Why are you leaving your current role?"

**Probing:** negativity risk — will you be describing THEM like this in 18 months?

**Strong shape:** pull, not push: the new thing you want (this team's problem, this scope) is the subject; the old thing gets one neutral sentence, zero venom. Managers pattern-match: the candidate who litigates their last manager is previewing your future 1:1s.

## M-10 — "What questions do you have for me?"

**Probing:** did you prepare, and will you manage up well?

**The manager-screen five (pick two or three):** "What does someone who excels here at 90 days look like versus someone who's just fine?" · "What's the risk you're most worried about on your roadmap this year?" · "How do decisions actually get made here — consensus, or someone's call?" · "What's the last thing you changed your mind about?" · "What would make you say in a year that hiring me was obviously right?" — every one of them makes the manager picture you in the role, which is the entire game.

---

**Prep protocol (one evening):** write your M-1 and M-4 stories fully (they're certainties); bullet the rest; rehearse the five questions out loud — managers remember candidates who interviewed THEM well.
