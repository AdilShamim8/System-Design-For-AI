# Flashcards — The Interview Process

24 cards. The game around the game: loops, screens, timelines, offers, and the meta-decisions.
---

**The standard AI-system-design loop — the five rounds.**

> Recruiter screen (fit, comp expectations) → technical phone (one design or coding, 45–60 min) → onsite: 3–5 rounds (system design, coding, ML breadth, behavioral, sometimes a takehome or specialized: eval/safety/platform) → team fit / values → offer. Know which round you're in; the signals differ.

**The phone screen's actual pass bar.**

> Not brilliance — *no strong-no signals*: can't structure a problem, can't take a hint, no numbers ever. Passing is avoiding disqualification; excelling starts the level-up conversation. Different games.

**Recruiter call: the two things to establish beyond fit.**

> (1) The loop's actual shape (rounds? takehome? who's the bar-raiser?) — it shapes your prep week; (2) comp expectations with a deflected-but-honest anchor ("competitive for the level; let's align after the process" beats a first number you can't take back).

**Takehome: the read-it-like-a-lawyer rule.**

> The spec's constraints are the grading rubric wearing a costume (10-takehome). Every 'must' and 'should' is a point; scope to hit all the musts before polishing any nice-to-have.

**The takehome defense call — what they're grading.**

> Not the artifact — your reasoning under questioning: why this scope, what you'd do with more time, what broke. Prepare three deep-dive threads you CHOOSE to be asked about.

**The 6-step framework (01) — name the steps.**

> (1) Clarify the job to be done · (2) constraints & scale · (3) the data/major components · (4) the model/pipeline choice with tradeoffs · (5) failure modes & evals · (6) numbers: latency, cost, quality. Interviewers recognize the shape; recognition reads as competence.

**The 'do you have questions?' moment — the strong three.**

> (1) What does the first 90 days look like for this role? (2) What's the biggest AI-systems risk on your roadmap? (3) How do evals gate releases here? — questions that make the interviewer see you in the role.

**The waiting-game timeline — what's normal and what's a flag.**

> Normal: decision within a week post-onsite, one follow-up nudge after that. Flag: silence past two weeks usually means (a) another candidate in flight, (b) headcount freeze, (c) process disfunction — ask directly; ambiguity past that point is your answer.

**Rejection with feedback vs without — the extraction protocol.**

> Without feedback: one polite ask for any signal, then move (most companies have a no-feedback policy; don't burn the bridge — re-application windows are 6–12 months). With feedback: thank, log it in your tracker, map it to a prep artifact and fix it before the next loop.

**Rejection-recovery: the 48-hour rule.**

> Feel it for 48 hours (it's information, not a verdict), then the debrief: what round, what question, what did I not have? One prep artifact per rejection — the log compounds (rejections file).

**Offer evaluation — the four dimensions.**

> (1) Cash (base + bonus), (2) equity (value, vesting, strike, last-round preference — treat paper money at a discount), (3) level & scope (what you'll own — the real comp), (4) market optionality (does this role make the next one bigger?).

**Negotiation: the one rule that matters.**

> Never negotiate against yourself: once you've named a number, wait for their response; counter-offers must be responsive to THEIR new terms, not your own restatement. Silence is a tool; the first person to fill it with a concession loses.

**Comp bands: the research stack (no exact numbers — the sources).**

> levels.fyi for self-reported tech comp, the RHT/Robert Half salary guides for broader market, Blind for unverified-but-directional culture/comp signal, and the posted-range laws (CO, CA, NY, WA) forcing disclosure in job posts. Triangulate; any single source lies.

**Leveling: the question to ask HR verbatim.**

> "What does the leveling rubric say distinguishes this level from the next one up?" — then interview AT that bar. Companies level on demonstrated scope, not years.

**The bar-raiser dynamic — what it means for you.**

> One interviewer (often outside the team) holds effectively a veto, calibrated across the org. Their signal: consistency of judgment across the whole loop. Practical implication: EVERY round is the round that matters; there's no 'safe' round to underperform.

**Multiple offers — the sequencing rule.**

> Slow the earliest, speed the latest: ask the early company for their best by date X (before your last onsite), never let an exploding offer compress your final loop — if it explodes, that's information about the culture.

**Team-choice over company-choice — the heuristic.**

> Company picks your resume's next line; team picks your next 2 years of skills, manager, and daily misery/joy. When offers are close, weight the manager and the mission of the specific team ~2x what most candidates do.

**The start-date negotiation — the most forgotten lever.**

> 2–4 weeks minimum between accept and start; 4–6 if a move is involved. A burned-out start (no break between jobs) costs more than any signing bonus delta; the company saying "yesterday" is testing boundaries you'll live under.

**Background/reference checks for AI roles — the delta.**

> Standard checks plus: publications/patents verification for research-flavored roles, and occasionally social-media screens for safety/customer-facing roles. References: prep three (one manager, one peer, one report if possible) with a one-pager per — make your champions' job easy.

**Interview burnout: the circuit-breaker.**

> More than 3 loops in 6 weeks degrades performance measurably (you start interviewing on fumes). Cap concurrent processes at 3; stagger starts; take one full rest day between onsites.

**The pipeline rule — the funnel math.**

> Realistic: ~30% recruiter-screen → onsite, ~20–30% onsite → offer. So ~10 loops per 1 offer at par. Run 5–8 concurrent processes at your target tier to make the funnel produce choices, not hopes.

**The 72-hour pre-onsite protocol — the three blocks.**

> (1) 24h out: company/product research — their AI surface (who's their AI for? what's the obvious failure mode?), one insight per interviewer; (2) same-day warm-up: one mock or two banks, NOT new material; (3) final 2h: logistics frozen, stories rehearsed once, sleep. The last 72 hours add zero knowledge and all the composure.

**The signal-reading cheat — how to know the onsite went well.**

> Positive tells: deep-dives getting deeper (they're enjoying it), spontaneous "here's what we do" architecture tours (they're imagining you in the room), the hiring manager talking team problems in detail. Negative: curveballs becoming walls of silence, time checks, early wrap.
